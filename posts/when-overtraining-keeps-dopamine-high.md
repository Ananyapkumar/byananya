## The Unexpected Brain Signal From Overtraining

A recent study from Brazil examined mice on an intense exercise regimen and found something a bit counter‑intuitive: dopamine, the neurotransmitter we usually associate with motivation and reward, stayed elevated even during the animals’ rest periods. In a normally balanced system, dopamine spikes during activity and falls back when you’re winding down. Here, the brain’s “off‑switch” didn’t quite work.

## Why It Catches My Eye

We all know a good run can feel rewarding because dopamine spikes, but the persistence of that spike after the workout ends is surprising. The researchers observed that the mice’s dopamine levels remained high when they should have been low, hinting that chronic, high‑intensity training can disrupt the usual regulatory cycle. If the same mechanism applies to people, it could help explain why some athletes report lingering anxiety, insomnia, or a constant sense of being on edge even on rest days.

## What This Means for Your Routine

- **Watch the volume**: Consistently pushing very high‑intensity sessions may warrant more low‑intensity or rest days to let your dopamine system reset.
- **Prioritize recovery**: Light stretching, meditation, or simply doing nothing isn’t just for sore muscles; it may give your brain a chance to bring dopamine back to baseline.
- **Listen to sleep cues**: Trouble falling asleep after a hard workout could signal that dopamine is still riding high. Scheduling the most demanding sessions earlier in the day can help.

Treating rest as a biological necessity rather than an optional luxury can keep both your muscles *and* your mind in better shape.
